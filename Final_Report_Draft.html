in Culturally Diverse Social Media Text

Course: CSCI 4055: Natural Language Processing  |  Ontario Tech University  |  April 2026

Abstract. We present a comprehensive evaluation of six large language models (LLMs) — three commercial API-based and three local open-weight — for depression detection in Arabic, Romanized Urdu, and Chinese social media posts. Using a balanced dataset of 5,000 samples per language (15,000 total), we run three experiments: (1) raw binary classification with culturally grounded V3 few-shot prompts, (2) keyword-level reasoning transparency where models identify the lexical signals behind their decisions, and (3) cross-lingual label consistency where models re-evaluate their classifications on English-translated versions of the same posts. Our results show that Romanized Urdu is easiest for all models (best F1: 0.9016, Gemini 2.0 Flash), Chinese is consistently hardest (best F1: 0.6606, Gemini 2.0 Flash), and Arabic lies in between (best F1: 0.8600, Gemini 2.0 Flash). Local open-weight models — particularly LLaMA 3 8B and Gemma 8B — match or approach commercial API performance on Arabic and Urdu, with F1 scores within 3–8 percentage points of the best commercial model. Keyword analysis reveals systematic patterns of semantic misalignment including negation confusion, cultural idiom misreading, and religious expression false positives. Cross-lingual consistency analysis (run for all models except Claude) shows DeepSeek 8B achieves the highest agreement rates on Arabic and Chinese (96%), followed by Gemini (84–94%), Gemma (88–92%), GPT-4o-mini (80–83%), and LLaMA (52–78%). Among online models, Gemini is substantially more self-consistent than GPT-4o-mini, suggesting Gemini's classifications are more robustly grounded in post content rather than surface language cues.

1. Introduction

Mental health detection in social media text has emerged as one of the most active applications of natural language processing, with the potential to assist clinical screening at population scale. However, the vast majority of existing work focuses on English-language data, leaving speakers of languages like Arabic (over 420 million speakers), Urdu (over 230 million), and Chinese (over 1.3 billion) severely underserved by current AI tools.

These languages present distinct and compounding challenges for LLMs. Arabic involves dialectal variation, rich morphology, and cultural norms around emotional expression tied to religious idiom and collectivist social values. Romanized Urdu involves code-switching between Roman script, native Urdu vocabulary, and English, encoded through poetic metaphors and culturally specific distress expressions. Chinese social media involves tonal ambiguity, regional variation, and culturally embedded expressions of distress that differ substantially from Western clinical vocabulary.

This report presents the final experimental results of our project, which systematically evaluates six LLMs — GPT-4o-mini, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Claude Haiku 4.5, meta-llama-3.1-8b-instruct, Gemma 8B, and deepseek-r1-0528-qwen3-8b — across three experiments spanning 90,000 individual classifications. Our primary contributions are:

  1. The largest comparative LLM evaluation for multilingual mental health classification to date, covering 6 models × 3 languages × 5,000 samples each (90,000 total classifications).
  2. A keyword transparency analysis that moves beyond accuracy metrics to surface the lexical signals driving model decisions — including systematic failure modes tied to cultural and linguistic nuance, and direct comparison of native-language vs. translated keyword behaviour.
  3. A cross-lingual label consistency analysis that tests whether model classifications are robust across language presentations of the same content, providing a novel measure of reasoning quality beyond correctness alone.
  4. Evidence that local open-weight models can match commercial API performance on some language-model combinations, with important implications for privacy-preserving mental health NLP deployment.

2. Background and Related Work

Earlier milestones in this project (Milestones 2 and 3) established the baseline methodology. Milestone 2 evaluated Gemini 2.0 Flash on Arabic using a 50-sample pilot, achieving 90.0% accuracy. Milestone 3 expanded to a 500-sample Urdu evaluation across four models and four prompting strategies, finding that: (a) V3 few-shot culturally grounded prompting outperformed zero-shot by 2.8–8.3%, (b) a translate-then-classify pipeline caused 8–14% accuracy drops relative to native-language processing, and (c) models exhibited distinct behavioral profiles — Gemini as a high-recall screener, GPT-4o-mini and DeepSeek 8B as balanced classifiers, and Claude Haiku 4.5 as an ultra-high-precision model that misses the majority of depressed posts.

The present report scales these findings to 5,000 samples per language and introduces Experiment 2 (keyword reasoning transparency) and Experiment 3 (cross-lingual consistency), enabling a much richer analysis of where and how model failures occur at the lexical and semantic level. Together, the three experiments provide classification accuracy, lexical attribution, and reasoning stability — three orthogonal dimensions of model quality that no prior multilingual mental health NLP evaluation has combined.

3. Data Preparation and Translation Pipeline

3.1 Dataset Sourcing

Three datasets were sourced, one per target language:

  • Arabic: CairoDep and related Arabic social media corpora, covering Egyptian dialect and Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) variation. Posts sourced from platforms commonly used in Arabic-speaking countries.
  • Romanized Urdu: The Roman Urdu Depression Assessment dataset (Mohmand et al.), a professionally curated academic dataset with four severity labels (non-depression, mild, moderate, severe) derived from social media platforms. Severity labels were collapsed into binary (depressed / not depressed) for this evaluation.
  • Chinese: Weibo-based mental health datasets reflecting Chinese social media expression patterns, sourced from Weibo (the dominant Chinese microblogging platform).

3.2 Translation and Cleaning

For each language, 6,000 samples were translated to English using Cohere's Command R model (command-r-08-2024), selected for its strong multilingual performance and competitive API costs. Translation served dual purposes: (1) as a data cleaning pass — posts that were ambiguous, corrupted, or off-topic were flagged and removed; and (2) producing the English translations used directly as input for Experiment 3. After cleaning, 5,000 samples were selected per language with a strict 50/50 depressed / not depressed stratified split.

5. Experimental Design

Experiment 1 — Multilingual Depression Classification (V3 Prompting)

All six models classified all 5,000 samples per language (15,000 per model, 90,000 total) using our V3 few-shot prompt strategy. V3 prompts include: multi-shot culturally grounded examples in the target language, exclusion rules for language-specific false positive patterns (poetry, religious expression, political commentary, casual usage), and a binary output constraint (Depressed / Not Depressed). Distinct V3 prompts were written for each language. This experiment produces standard classification metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1) for every model–language combination and serves as the primary quantitative result of the project.

Experiment 2 — Keyword Extraction and Reasoning Transparency

Each model received the same post it classified in Experiment 1 along with its own assigned label, and was asked to identify 1–2 keywords most influential in its decision, plus their English translations. This experiment probes reasoning at the lexical level — surfacing which words drove each classification and whether those words reflect genuine clinical signals or systematic biases. Experiment 2 uses the same labels as Experiment 1 (it does not re-classify), so its metrics are identical to Experiment 1 by design; the analytical value lies in the keyword output, not the classification outcome.

Experiment 3 — Cross-Lingual Label Consistency

Each model received the Cohere-translated English version of the post it classified in Experiment 1, along with its original label. It was asked: does it still agree with this classification? It also identified 1–2 English keywords central to this re-evaluation. This tests whether model decisions are driven by post content (language-invariant) or surface-level language cues. A model that changes its decision when shown the translated version is exhibiting cross-lingual inconsistency, raising questions about the robustness of its reasoning. Experiment 3 was run for all models except Claude (5 of 6), across all three languages.

6.4 Key Findings from Experiment 1

Chinese is Consistently the Most Difficult Language

All six models show substantially lower F1 on Chinese compared to Arabic and Urdu. The best Chinese result — Gemini at F1=0.6606 — falls well below the best Arabic result (Gemini at 0.8600) and the best Urdu result (Gemini at 0.9016). This likely reflects the relative scarcity of Chinese mental health NLP data in model pretraining corpora, combined with the cultural distance between Chinese social media expression conventions and Western clinical vocabulary. A consistent pattern across all Chinese results is very high precision but very low recall: models are reluctant to classify Chinese posts as depressed, leading to many missed cases (false negatives). On average across all models, recall on Chinese is 0.38 versus 0.74 on Arabic and 0.76 on Urdu.

Local Open-Weight Models Are Competitive with Commercial APIs

A critical finding for practical deployment: LLaMA 3 8B achieves F1=0.8630 on Urdu (vs. Gemini's 0.9016 and GPT-4o-mini's 0.8864) and F1=0.8255 on Arabic (vs. Gemini's 0.8600). Gemma 8B similarly achieves F1=0.8722 on Urdu and 0.8302 on Arabic. These results demonstrate that, for Arabic and Urdu at least, local models running on consumer hardware can provide classification quality approaching the best commercial APIs — a compelling finding for privacy-preserving deployment in clinical or social work contexts where data sovereignty is important.

Claude Haiku 4.5: The Ultra-High-Precision Anomaly

Claude Haiku 4.5 exhibits an extreme precision-recall imbalance across all languages, particularly on Chinese (precision=0.932, recall=0.236, F1=0.377) and Urdu (precision=0.980, recall=0.420, F1=0.588). Claude generates almost no false positives — it virtually never incorrectly flags a non-depressed post as depressed — but it misses the majority of depressed posts. On Chinese, it correctly identifies only 591 of 2,500 depressed posts (23.6%). This pattern appears to reflect safety alignment training that produces a strong prior against flagging content as indicative of mental health distress, even when clear clinical signals are present. For depression screening applications where missing a depressed individual is far more costly than a false alarm, Claude Haiku 4.5 is not suitable as a primary classifier.

GPT-4o-mini: High Precision, Low Recall Pattern

GPT-4o-mini also shows a consistent precision-over-recall bias: on Arabic (precision=0.966, recall=0.626), it correctly identifies almost all posts it classifies as depressed, but misses 936 of 2,500 genuinely depressed posts. This conservative behavior was documented in Milestone 2 and persists at scale. On Urdu, GPT-4o-mini achieves near-parity with Gemini (F1=0.886 vs. 0.902), but its Chinese performance (F1=0.474) reveals how this conservative prior becomes catastrophic when the model lacks strong language-specific signal.

DeepSeek 8B Underperforms Relative to Other Local Models

Despite being described as "multilingual-tuned," DeepSeek 8B consistently underperforms LLaMA 3 8B and Gemma 8B. On Urdu, it achieves only F1=0.677 — well below LLaMA (0.863) and Gemma (0.872). On Chinese, it achieves F1=0.516, above Gemma (0.444) and Claude (0.377) but below LLaMA (0.604). On Arabic it also falls below both LLaMA and Gemma. This suggests that the multilingual tuning in this DeepSeek variant does not generalize well to the languages and domains represented in our evaluation.

7.5 Qualitative Examples: Semantic Misalignment in Keywords

Keyword extraction enables a form of error analysis that would otherwise require native speaker annotation: when a model misclassifies a post, the keywords it cites reveal why. Below we highlight selected examples where keyword evidence reveals systematic failure modes.

Example A — Arabic False Negative: Denial Language Overrides Content (GPT-4o-mini)

Language: Arabic  |  GT: Depressed  |  Predicted: Not Depressed
Exp2 Keyword (translated): lost
Analysis: The post describes internal disorientation — "Don't call me depressed because I am lost, not depressed." The model extracted "lost" as its keyword, correctly identifying the distress signal at the lexical level. However, the explicit self-denial of the depression label ("I am not depressed") appears to have overridden the clinical significance of "lost" as a distress marker. This is a psychologically nuanced failure: the speaker's cognitive denial of their own depressive state is characteristic of depressive cognition, yet the model treats the surface negation as the authoritative signal.

Example B — Arabic False Positive: Social Content Without Distress (GPT-4o-mini)

Language: Arabic  |  GT: Not Depressed  |  Predicted: Depressed
Exp2 Keywords (translated): lonely, I can't, I have no, no one
Analysis: The post contains multiple phrases that pattern-match to isolation and helplessness vocabulary. However, the ground truth is not depressed — the post likely represents a momentary social complaint or casual expression. This false positive illustrates how surface-level keyword matching on isolation vocabulary can produce false alarms when posts lack the persistent, clinical-level distress that annotators recognized as absent.

Example C — Arabic Religious Expression: Correct Handling (Gemini)

Language: Arabic  |  GT: Not Depressed  |  Predicted: Not Depressed
Exp2 Keyword (translated): O God, forgive
Analysis: The model correctly identified a post containing Islamic prayer expressions and classified it as not depressed. The keyword "O God, forgive" — a common Arabic supplication — was appropriately recognized as religious expression rather than despair. This represents a successful application of the V3 prompt's religious expression exclusion rule, reflected in the high global frequency of "gratitude."

Example D — Urdu False Negative: Semantic Collapse on Distress Keywords (Claude)

Language: Romanized Urdu  |  GT: Depressed  |  Predicted: Not Depressed
Exp2 Keyword (translated): exhaustion, sleep
Analysis: The model extracted "exhaustion" and "sleep" from a post describing behavioral depression symptoms (fatigue, sleep disruption). Yet the classification is "Not Depressed." Consistent with Claude's documented ultra-conservative behavior: even when the model identifies clinically relevant distress signals in the keywords, its safety prior overrides the clinical inference. Claude's problem is not keyword recognition failure — it is that keyword signals are insufficient to overcome its alignment-driven reluctance to label content as depression.

Example E — Urdu False Negative: Poetry Exclusion Rule Misapplied (Claude)

Language: Romanized Urdu  |  GT: Depressed  |  Predicted: Not Depressed
Exp2 Keyword (translated): poetry
Analysis: The model cited "poetry" as the primary keyword justifying a "Not Depressed" classification. The V3 prompt includes an exclusion rule for poetry and song lyrics — designed for formal literary expression, not personal posts that happen to use poetic register to express genuine suffering. The model appears to have over-applied the poetry exclusion, suppressing depression classification for any post with poetic language regardless of clinical content. This mirrors the most persistent failure mode identified in Milestone 3.

8.2 Key Findings from Experiment 3

DeepSeek Achieves Highest Cross-Lingual Consistency on Arabic and Chinese

Among all models with valid results (excluding DeepSeek and Gemma on Urdu where the run failed after fewer than 15 samples), DeepSeek 8B achieves the highest agreement rates on both Arabic (96.19%) and Chinese (96.07%). This is notably higher than Gemini (93.84% / 93.21%) and GPT-4o-mini (82.60% / 82.19%). DeepSeek's very low flip rates (3.18–3.34%) suggest its classifications are highly stable across language presentations — though whether this reflects robust semantic grounding or a narrow decision boundary that is hard to shift is worth further investigation.

Gemini More Self-Consistent than GPT-4o-mini Among Online Models

Among the two online models with complete valid results, Gemini upholds its original classifications 83.84–93.84% of the time, compared to 79.72–82.60% for GPT-4o-mini. The gap is most pronounced on Arabic (Gemini: 93.84% vs. GPT-4o-mini: 82.60%) and Chinese (93.21% vs. 82.19%). GPT-4o-mini's lower consistency indicates that approximately 17–20% of its classifications may be influenced by language-specific surface patterns rather than underlying semantic content.

LLaMA Shows Substantially Lower Cross-Lingual Consistency

LLaMA 3 8B has the lowest agreement rates among all models with valid results: 78.15% on Arabic, 63.72% on Urdu, and only 52.41% on Chinese. A 52.41% agreement rate on Chinese is barely above chance — meaning LLaMA's Exp1 Chinese classifications are nearly indistinguishable from random re-labelling when the same content is presented in English. This is consistent with LLaMA's classification performance in Exp1 (F1=0.604 on Chinese, the best local model but still weak) and suggests that LLaMA's Chinese decisions rely heavily on surface language features that do not survive translation.

Urdu Shows the Most Disagreement for Online Models

Both Gemini and GPT-4o-mini show higher flip rates on Urdu (16.14% and 20.26%) than on Arabic or Chinese. For LLaMA, the flip rate on Urdu (34.20%) is also lower than on Chinese (43.58%), but Urdu still shows substantial instability. This may reflect transliteration loss in the Cohere translations — poetic expressions and culturally embedded Urdu idioms may not survive translation as faithfully as Arabic or Chinese text.

8.3 The Four-Category Flip Analysis

Experiment 3 disagreements can be decomposed into four clinically meaningful categories based on the model's original correctness (Exp1) and whether it changes its decision (Exp3):

  • A: Correct + Upheld (Confirmed correct) — Model was right originally and stays right on translation. Best case.
  • B: Correct + Overruled (Harmful flip) — Model was right originally but changes to the wrong label on translation. The translation destabilised a correct decision.
  • C: Wrong + Corrected (Beneficial flip) — Model was wrong originally but self-corrects when shown the translation. The translation improved the decision.
  • D: Wrong + Upheld (Confirmed wrong) — Model was wrong originally and remains wrong. The translation did not help.

10. Discussion

10.1 Overall Reliability and Trust: A Framework

Across all three experiments, a consistent picture emerges that can be summarised in two complementary dimensions: reliability (how well the model classifies) and trust (how robustly and consistently it reasons). The figures below visualise where each model falls on these two axes.

The trust quadrant makes the trade-off landscape concrete: Gemini occupies the high-accuracy, high-consistency quadrant; GPT-4o-mini achieves high precision but lower consistency; DeepSeek and Gemma show high cross-lingual consistency on Arabic and Chinese despite lower overall classification F1. LLaMA shows the lowest consistency, especially on Chinese. Claude was excluded from Exp3 — its extreme precision-recall imbalance (precision near 0.98, recall near 0.42 on Urdu) places it in an anomalous corner: highly specific but clinically unreliable for depression screening.

10.2 Commercial APIs vs. Local Open-Weight Models

The results provide nuanced evidence for the viability of local open-weight models as privacy-preserving alternatives to commercial APIs. For Arabic and Urdu, LLaMA 3 8B and Gemma 8B achieve F1 scores within 3–8 percentage points of the best commercial API (Gemini 2.0 Flash), running entirely on local hardware with no data transmission to external servers. This makes them viable candidates for deployment in clinical settings with data sovereignty requirements or in low-resource environments where API costs are prohibitive.

However, for Chinese, the gap widens substantially: the best local model (LLaMA, F1=0.604) lags Gemini (0.661) by 5.7 points, and all models struggle. This likely reflects both the inherent difficulty of Chinese mental health NLP and the relative underrepresentation of Chinese mental health vocabulary in local model pretraining. DeepSeek 8B's underperformance relative to LLaMA and Gemma is unexpected given its "multilingual-tuned" designation — the specific languages and domains it was tuned on may not include the dialectal variants and domains in our datasets.

10.3 The Precision-Recall Trade-off in Clinical Context

The clinical implications of the precision-recall trade-off are significant. In a depression screening context, false negatives (missed depression) are far more costly than false positives (false alarms): a false negative means a person in distress receives no intervention, while a false positive means an unnecessary but not harmful follow-up. Under this asymmetric cost structure:

  • Gemini 2.0 Flash (high recall across languages) is the best-suited model for initial screening, where the priority is maximising sensitivity at the cost of some false alarms.
  • LLaMA 3 8B (recall=0.922 on Urdu) offers screening-quality performance in a fully local deployment — the best privacy-preserving screening option available.
  • Claude Haiku 4.5 is entirely unsuitable for screening — its recall of 23.6% on Chinese means it would miss 76.4% of depressed Chinese posts in any deployment.
  • GPT-4o-mini is suitable for secondary confirmation (high precision) but not primary screening, given its conservative classification bias.

10.4 Cultural and Linguistic Failure Modes

The keyword analysis surfaces several recurring failure modes that are cultural and linguistic in origin, not merely statistical:

  1. Denial language as dominant signal: Posts where speakers explicitly deny being depressed trigger negative classifications even when the surrounding content expresses genuine distress. The model anchors on the explicit negation rather than reading the clinical subtext.
  2. Religious expression false suppression: Islamic and other religious expressions (prayer, acceptance of fate, gratitude) appear to suppress depression classifications even in posts with genuine distress signals. The V3 prompt's religious expression exclusion rule helps but cannot fully resolve this — the boundary between religious acceptance and resigned hopelessness is semantically subtle.
  3. Poetry exclusion over-generalisation: Models trained with the V3 "do not classify poetry as depressed" rule appear to apply this broadly to any post with poetic register, even when the content expresses genuine personal suffering. Distinguishing literary convention from clinically significant poetic expression requires cultural knowledge that current LLMs partially lack.
  4. Chinese cultural vocabulary gap: Chinese social media expressions of distress — particularly indirect, metaphorical, or euphemistic references to depression — appear to be systematically under-detected. Models trained primarily on Western clinical vocabulary lack sufficient exposure to the Chinese cultural vocabulary of distress.

10.5 Cross-Lingual Consistency as a Measure of Reasoning Quality

Experiment 3 introduces a novel evaluation dimension: not just what a model decides but how stable that decision is when the same content is presented in a different language. Among all models with valid Exp3 results, DeepSeek 8B achieves the highest consistency on Arabic and Chinese (96%), followed by Gemini (84–94%), Gemma (88–92% on Arabic/Chinese), GPT-4o-mini (80–82%), LLaMA (52–78%), and — excluded from Exp3 — Claude. The spread is wide: LLaMA's Chinese agreement rate of 52% means it is effectively re-randomising its classification when shown a translation of the same post.

Among online models specifically, Gemini's higher cross-lingual consistency (83–94%) compared to GPT-4o-mini (80–83%) suggests that Gemini's decisions are more deeply grounded in semantic content. A model that changes its mind 17–20% of the time when shown a translation of the same post is exhibiting reasoning instability that raises legitimate questions about deployment reliability, even when its aggregate accuracy appears high.

The four-category flip analysis further refines this picture: beneficial flips (wrong-to-right) are valuable because translation provides additional disambiguation; harmful flips (right-to-wrong) represent cases where translation introduced new confusion. A model with more harmful than beneficial flips is not merely unstable — it is being actively misled by the translation, suggesting its original reasoning was more aligned with the native language's surface features than with the underlying semantic content.

11. Conclusion

This project presents the most comprehensive comparative evaluation of LLMs for multilingual depression detection to date, covering six models across three languages and three experimental paradigms. Our key findings are:

  1. Chinese is the hardest language for all models. All six models show substantially lower F1 on Chinese than on Arabic or Urdu, with a consistent pattern of high precision but very low recall — models are reluctant to classify Chinese posts as depressed, missing the majority of true cases. The average recall on Chinese (0.38) is roughly half that on Arabic (0.74) and Urdu (0.76).
  2. Local open-weight models can match commercial APIs on Arabic and Urdu. LLaMA 3 8B and Gemma 8B achieve competitive F1 scores (within 3–8 points of Gemini 2.0 Flash) on both Arabic and Urdu, making them viable privacy-preserving alternatives for deployment in sensitive healthcare contexts where data sovereignty is required.
  3. Claude Haiku 4.5's safety alignment makes it unsuitable for depression screening. Its extreme precision-recall imbalance (recall as low as 23.6% on Chinese) means it would miss the vast majority of depressed posts — an unacceptable outcome for any screening application.
  4. Keyword analysis reveals systematic semantic misalignment. Models consistently misclassify posts involving denial language, religious expression, poetic register, and culturally embedded Chinese distress vocabulary — failure modes that are invisible in aggregate metrics but clearly visible in per-prediction keyword evidence. Native-language keyword analysis confirms models engage directly with the original-language vocabulary rather than relying on internal translation.
  5. DeepSeek shows the highest cross-lingual consistency; LLaMA the lowest. Among models with valid Exp3 results, DeepSeek 8B achieves 96% agreement on Arabic and Chinese, while LLaMA 3 8B falls to 52% on Chinese — barely above chance. Among the two online models, Gemini (83–94% agreement) is more self-consistent than GPT-4o-mini (80–83%). Keyword Jaccard similarity analysis confirms Gemini preserves more of its lexical focus across language presentations than GPT-4o-mini.
  6. Culturally grounded prompting is essential but insufficient. The V3 few-shot approach with language-specific examples and exclusion rules substantially improved performance over zero-shot baselines, but systematic errors persist that prompt engineering alone cannot resolve without deeper cultural and clinical knowledge.

These findings have direct practical implications for practitioners building multilingual mental health NLP tools. For screening applications, Gemini 2.0 Flash offers the best combination of high recall and cross-lingual stability. For privacy-preserving deployments, LLaMA 3 8B provides the best local model performance on Arabic and Urdu. For Chinese, all current models are insufficient for reliable clinical screening and should be augmented with human review. Future work should pursue Chinese-specific culturally grounded prompt engineering, ensemble approaches combining the complementary error profiles of Gemini (low FN) and GPT-4o-mini (low FP), and manual annotation by native-speaking mental health professionals to validate ground truth labels and categorise error types.
